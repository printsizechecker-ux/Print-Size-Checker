Affinity vs Photoshop: The Ultimate Comparison

Written by Marcus Webb Published April 14, 2026 · 8 min read

For over three decades, Adobe Photoshop has reigned as the undisputed king of image editing. It has become so synonymous with photo manipulation that "photoshopping" entered the dictionary as a verb. But in 2025, the landscape shifted dramatically when Canva acquired Serif and made Affinity Photo completely free. This development fundamentally changes the value proposition for photographers, designers, and creative professionals worldwide.

The comprehensive comparison in the video above puts both applications through real-world tests, examining not just feature lists but actual workflow efficiency, output quality, and long-term viability. This guide expands on that analysis, providing you with the definitive resource for choosing between the industry standard and the disruptive challenger.

1. The Pricing Revolution: Free vs Subscription

The most striking difference between Affinity and Photoshop is the cost model. Adobe Photoshop operates exclusively on a subscription basis through Creative Cloud. The Photography Plan, which includes Photoshop and Lightroom, costs $9.99 monthly—$119.88 annually, $599.40 over five years, and nearly $1,200 over a decade of use. Stop paying, and the software stops working.

Affinity Photo, by contrast, is now completely free. Following Canva's acquisition of Serif, the entire Affinity suite (Photo, Designer, and Publisher) was rebranded as Affinity Studio and made available at zero cost. This is not a trial, a student version, or a feature-limited edition. The full professional application, with every tool and capability, costs nothing.

The financial reality: For hobbyists, students, and emerging professionals, the difference is transformative. The money saved on Adobe subscriptions can fund camera equipment, printing supplies, educational resources, or simply remain in your bank account. For established professionals, the calculation differs—Photoshop's ecosystem and AI features may justify the subscription cost, but the free alternative now demands serious consideration.

2. Interface and Learning Curve

Both applications present professional-grade interfaces that assume familiarity with layer-based editing, masking, and non-destructive workflows. Neither is "easy" in the way consumer mobile apps are easy. Both require investment in learning.

Photoshop's interface has evolved over three decades into a dense, powerful, occasionally overwhelming workspace. Panels, tools, and options proliferate. The learning curve is steep but rewarded with unmatched depth. Adobe has improved accessibility through AI-assisted tools that reduce manual steps, making common tasks feel less intimidating for beginners.

Affinity's interface deliberately mirrors Photoshop's layout to minimize transition friction—the toolbar on the left, properties on top, layers on the right. However, Affinity introduces the "Persona" system: separate workspaces for different tasks (Photo, Liquify, Develop, Tone Mapping, Export). This separation reduces clutter but initially confuses users expecting a single unified workspace. The recent Affinity 3 update reworked the interface, potentially confusing users following older tutorials.

Learning resources: Photoshop benefits from 30+ years of tutorials, courses, books, and community knowledge. Every question has been answered somewhere. Affinity's learning ecosystem is smaller but growing, with Serif providing built-in tutorials through the Affinity Portal. However, much third-party content still references older versions, creating friction for new users.

3. Core Editing Capabilities

For fundamental photo editing—exposure correction, color grading, retouching, compositing—both applications deliver professional results. The differences emerge in workflow efficiency and specialized features.

RAW Processing: Photoshop uses Adobe Camera Raw (ACR), a mature, feature-rich engine shared with Lightroom. ACR provides comprehensive exposure control, color management, lens corrections, noise reduction, and detail enhancement. Affinity's Develop Persona offers similar controls but feels less refined and more fragmented compared to ACR's polished integration.

Layers and Masking: Both offer full non-destructive editing with adjustment layers, blend modes, and masks. Affinity's layers behave like Photoshop's Smart Objects by default—you can resize repeatedly without quality loss. Affinity's "Live Filters" allow non-destructive filter application that remains editable, comparable to Photoshop's Smart Filters.

Selection Tools: Photoshop's AI-powered Select Subject, Select Sky, and Object Selection provide one-click isolation of complex elements. Affinity's Object Selection offers edge-aware detection but lacks Photoshop's AI sophistication. For complex cutouts—hair, fur, translucent fabrics—Photoshop's tools save significant time.

Retouching: Both provide Clone Stamp, Healing Brush, Patch tools, Frequency Separation, and Dodge & Burn. Affinity actually includes Frequency Separation as a built-in macro, while Photoshop requires manual setup or third-party plugins. For skin retouching and detailed correction, both satisfy professional standards.

4. Where Photoshop Pulls Ahead

Despite Affinity's impressive capabilities, Adobe maintains clear advantages in several critical areas:

  • AI-Powered Features: Generative Fill, Generative Expand, Content-Aware Fill, and Neural Filters represent genuine workflow revolution. Removing distractions, extending backgrounds, or adding elements with text prompts saves hours compared to manual techniques. Affinity lacks comparable AI tools—the Canva Pro integration offers some AI features but requires separate subscription and lacks Photoshop's seamless integration.
  • Content-Aware Technology: Photoshop's Content-Aware Fill intelligently replaces selected areas with contextually appropriate content. The Remove Tool (new in 2024-2025) makes object deletion as simple as brushing over the distraction. Affinity offers Inpainting and cloning but nothing approaching this level of automated intelligence.
  • Ecosystem Integration: Photoshop integrates seamlessly with Lightroom, Illustrator, After Effects, and the broader Creative Cloud. Files, settings, and assets synchronize across devices. Cloud collaboration enables team workflows. Affinity is standalone software without ecosystem benefits.
  • Plugin Architecture: Thousands of third-party plugins, extensions, and panels extend Photoshop's capabilities. Specialized tools for portrait retouching, landscape enhancement, and workflow automation abound. Affinity supports some Photoshop plugins but the ecosystem remains comparatively limited.
  • Industry Standard: Photoshop's PSD format is the universal language of creative professionals. Printers, agencies, and collaborators expect PSD files. While Affinity opens and exports PSDs, complex layer effects, smart objects, and text styles may not transfer perfectly.

5. Where Affinity Surprises

Affinity is not merely a Photoshop clone at a lower price. Several features demonstrate independent innovation:

  • Haze Removal: Affinity's Haze filter surpasses Photoshop and Lightroom's equivalent. The Distance parameter allows selective haze removal—clearing foreground haze while preserving atmospheric perspective in backgrounds. The results show fewer color artifacts and halos compared to Adobe's implementation.
  • Performance: Affinity consistently outperforms Photoshop in rendering speed, startup time, and memory efficiency. It runs smoothly on modest hardware where Photoshop might lag. File operations feel snappier, and the application rarely crashes even with complex documents.
  • Live Filters: All filters in Affinity can be applied as non-destructive "Live Filters" that remain editable indefinitely. While Photoshop offers Smart Filters, Affinity's implementation feels more integrated and flexible.
  • Persona System: Separating tasks into dedicated workspaces (Photo, Liquify, Develop, Tone Mapping, Export) reduces interface clutter. Each persona presents only relevant tools, streamlining workflow once you adapt to the paradigm.
  • File System Integration: Unlike Lightroom's catalog system or Photoshop's proprietary formats, Affinity works directly with your file system. No databases to corrupt, no "missing file" references, no import/export rituals. Your folder structure is your organization system.
  • Infinite Undo: Affinity remembers every action back to document creation. No history panel limitations, no accidental permanent changes.

6. Performance and Hardware Requirements

Affinity Photo is notably lighter on system resources. It launches faster, uses less RAM, and maintains responsiveness on older or less powerful hardware. This efficiency makes it attractive for mobile professionals, students with budget laptops, or anyone prioritizing speed over feature density.

Photoshop, while more demanding, scales better with high-end hardware. Complex operations on large files—extensive layering, 3D work, video editing—benefit from powerful processors and abundant RAM. The AI features require modern hardware acceleration.

iPad Performance: Affinity Photo for iPad offers nearly complete feature parity with the desktop version. The application feels native and responsive on Apple Silicon. Photoshop for iPad remains limited compared to desktop, lacking many professional features despite gradual improvements.

7. File Compatibility and Workflow Integration

Photoshop's PSD format is the industry standard. Every professional printer, design agency, and creative collaborator understands PSD. Layered files, masks, adjustment layers, and smart objects transfer seamlessly within the Adobe ecosystem.

Affinity opens PSD files preserving most layers, masks, and adjustments. However, complex smart objects, certain text styles, and advanced layer effects may rasterize or simplify. Exporting to PSD from Affinity works for basic documents but may flatten sophisticated compositions.

Affinity's native .afphoto format is not recognized by Adobe applications. Collaboration with Photoshop users requires PSD export, potentially losing editability. For solo practitioners or teams standardized on Affinity, this limitation is irrelevant. For mixed environments or client deliverables, it creates friction.

8. The Verdict: Who Should Choose What

Choose Affinity Photo if:

  • You reject subscription models on principle or budget
  • You primarily need photo editing without AI dependency
  • You value performance and efficiency over feature density
  • You work independently without extensive collaboration
  • You prefer file system organization over database catalogs
  • You need capable iPad editing with desktop parity

Choose Photoshop if:

  • You rely on AI features for productivity (Generative Fill, Neural Filters)
  • You collaborate with Adobe-centric teams or clients
  • You depend on plugin ecosystems for specialized workflows
  • You require seamless cloud synchronization across devices
  • You work in an agency or corporate environment standardized on Adobe
  • You need video editing, 3D, or advanced compositing capabilities

9. The Hybrid Approach

Many photographers settle on a hybrid workflow: maintaining Affinity for routine editing and Photoshop subscription for projects requiring AI features or specific plugins. This "Adobe vacation" model maximizes capability while minimizing subscription duration.

Others use Bridge (free) and Camera Raw (free) for raw processing and organization, then Affinity for detailed editing—achieving professional results without any Adobe subscription at all.

The choice is no longer binary. With Affinity now free, experimentation costs nothing. You can maintain both applications, choosing the right tool for each project rather than forcing every workflow through a single paradigm.

10. The Future Landscape

Canva's acquisition of Serif and Affinity's subsequent free release represents strategic disruption. By making professional tools accessible to everyone, Canva challenges Adobe's monopoly and builds a pathway from simple design tools to professional workflows.

Adobe continues accelerating AI integration, betting that generative features justify subscription costs. Photoshop becomes increasingly powerful for those embracing AI-assisted workflows, potentially widening the capability gap with Affinity.

For photographers, this competition benefits everyone. Adobe faces pressure to justify its pricing. Affinity gains resources for continued development. Users gain genuine choice—between subscription and ownership, between AI assistance and traditional technique, between ecosystem integration and standalone independence.

The ultimate winner is not the software that dominates the market, but the creative who chooses tools aligned with their values, workflow, and budget. In 2025, that choice has never been more genuinely available.
